# Section 2 — GPU architecture — slides and script

Five slides, ~10 minutes, for an audience that knows hardware but has never
written a kernel. `[PAUSE]` = stop talking for two seconds.

**Vocabulary rule: no term is used before the slide that defines it.** Slide 1
deliberately says nothing about warps, SMs or CUDA cores.

Sources for pictures:
- **[WP]** = NVIDIA *H100 / Hopper Architecture Whitepaper*
- **[PG]** = NVIDIA *CUDA C++ Programming Guide* (has the classic CPU-vs-GPU figure)
- **[OURS]** = generated by this repo — `python3 draw_arch.py`

## Slide 1 — What's inside the thing that just did that?

**Picture:** the classic CPU-vs-GPU transistor-budget diagram — **[PG]** "The GPU
Devotes More Transistors to Data Processing". Two blocks side by side: the CPU
mostly cache and control with a handful of ALUs, the GPU almost entirely ALUs.

*Fallback if you'd rather use real silicon:* an H100 die shot **[WP]** beside any
desktop CPU die shot. The visual point is the same — the GPU is a repeated tile,
the CPU is not.

**Bullets**
- CPU: 14 big cores. Most of the die is **cache, branch prediction, out-of-order**
- GPU: many small units. Most of the die is **arithmetic and registers**
- Comparable transistor budgets. Opposite decision.
- CPU makes **one** thread fast. GPU runs **many** threads at once.

**Script** *(~110 s)*

> So let's look at what actually did that. And I want to start with one design
> decision, because everything else in this section follows from it.
>
> [PAUSE — let them look at the two pictures]
>
> These two chips have comparable transistor counts. On the left, the CPU: most of
> that area is not arithmetic at all. It's cache, branch predictors, out-of-order
> scheduling machinery. All of it exists to make a *single* instruction stream run
> as fast as physically possible.
>
> On the right, the GPU. Almost all of that area is arithmetic units and
> registers. Very little of it is spent making any one thread fast — and it
> shows. An individual GPU thread is *slower* than a CPU thread. Lower clock, no
> speculation, no out-of-order execution.
>
> [PAUSE]
>
> Same transistor budget. Opposite decision. The CPU optimises one worker very
> hard. The GPU gives up on that entirely and runs thousands of mediocre ones.
>
> And notice the GPU is visibly a repeated tile. That repetition is the
> architecture — so let's name the parts.

## Slide 2 — The parts, with numbers

**Picture:** two panels from **[WP]** — the full-chip diagram (the grid of SMs)
and the single-SM block diagram beside it. Draw the audience's eye from one to
the other; that pairing *is* the slide.

**Bullets**

| level | what it is | [WP] |
|---|---|---|
| **thread** | one lane of arithmetic — one node in our kernel | — |
| **warp** | **32 threads that execute together, always** | fixed at 32 |
| **SM** | the real hardware unit: schedulers, registers, L1/shared | ~132 per H100 |
| **grid** | your whole launch, streamed across all SMs | millions of threads |

**Script** *(~110 s)*

> Four words of vocabulary, and then we're done with definitions.
>
> A **thread** is one lane of arithmetic. In our kernel, one thread computes one
> node's arrival time. That's the whole mapping.
>
> A **warp** is thirty-two threads that execute *together*. Not "can execute
> together" — always. Hold on to that, it's the next slide.
>
> An **SM**, a streaming multiprocessor, is the actual hardware unit. It has warp
> schedulers, a register file, and a scratchpad memory. The closest CPU analogy is
> a core — except this core keeps dozens of warps resident simultaneously. An
> H100 has about a hundred and thirty-two of them.
>
> And a **grid** is your whole launch. When we ran eight million nodes in section
> one, that was eight million threads. You don't schedule them; the hardware
> streams them through the SMs.
>
> [PAUSE]
>
> Here's the comparison that matters. A CPU core runs one thread, switches maybe
> every millisecond, and the switch costs microseconds — save the registers, save
> the stack, into the kernel and back. An SM keeps dozens of warps resident and
> switches between them *every cycle*, for free.
>
> That difference is the entire architecture. Everything after this is a
> consequence of it.

## Slide 3 — SIMT: 32 threads, one instruction

**Picture:** **[OURS]** `figures/09_simt_coalescing.png`. Top half: one
instruction fanning out to 32 lanes. Bottom half: the same 32 lanes reading
consecutive addresses (one wide transaction) versus scattered addresses (32
separate ones).

This is the alien idea. Spend the time; it also earns you coalescing, which you
need in section 3.

**Bullets**
- One instruction is issued to **all 32 lanes at once**
- You write scalar code for one node; hardware runs 32 nodes in lockstep
- Lanes disagree on a branch → **both sides execute**, wrong lanes idle
- Lanes read scattered addresses → **one load becomes 32 loads**

**Script** *(~120 s)*

> This is the part with no CPU equivalent, so I'll go slowly.
>
> The hardware fetches *one* instruction and issues it to all thirty-two lanes of
> the warp at the same time. You write ordinary scalar code — "compute node i" —
> and thirty-two copies of it run in lockstep on thirty-two different nodes.
>
> [PAUSE]
>
> That's the bargain, and it has two consequences you cannot escape.
>
> First: if those thirty-two lanes hit a branch and disagree — some take it, some
> don't — the hardware executes *both* sides, with the inactive lanes switched
> off. You pay for both paths. That's divergence.
>
> Second, and for our workload this is the important one. If the thirty-two lanes
> ask for thirty-two *consecutive* addresses, the memory system merges them into
> one wide transaction. If they ask for thirty-two scattered addresses, you get
> thirty-two separate transactions — and roughly a thirty-second of the bandwidth.
>
> [PAUSE]
>
> That's coalescing. And it means data layout on a GPU is a performance decision,
> not a matter of taste.
>
> It's exactly why our benchmark stores fanin slot *k* contiguously across all
> nodes, instead of keeping each node's eight edges together. Adjacent threads
> then read adjacent addresses, and one instruction produces one transaction.

## Slide 4 — So why is the bandwidth 9× higher?

**Picture:** **[OURS]** `figures/10_latency_hiding.png`. Left: two timelines — a
CPU thread issuing a load and stalling, against an SM with many warps where a
stall simply switches to another warp. Right: the Little's Law table.

**Bullets**
- The boring half: the bus is wider — 128-bit DDR5 → 256-bit GDDR6 → HBM3
- **The surprise: GPU memory latency is *worse* than a CPU's**
- `bytes in flight = bandwidth × latency` → CPU ~4 KB, A4000 ~200 KB, **H100 ~2 MB**
- A CPU core tracks ~a dozen outstanding misses. It *cannot* hold 2 MB in the air.
- The GPU doesn't avoid latency. It **hides** it.

**Script** *(~150 s)*

> Now we can answer the question this section is really about. Why does a GPU move
> data nine times faster?
>
> Half the answer is boring: the pipe is physically wider. Dual-channel DDR5 is a
> hundred-and-twenty-eight-bit interface. The A4000 is two-fifty-six-bit GDDR6.
> An H100 has HBM stacked on the package with a bus thousands of bits wide. You
> paid for that; there's no trick.
>
> But a wide pipe is worthless unless you can keep it full. And here's the part
> that surprises people.
>
> [PAUSE]
>
> GPU memory latency is *worse* than a CPU's. Several hundred nanoseconds, against
> about ninety. The GPU is not faster at answering any single memory request. It
> is meaningfully slower.
>
> [PAUSE]
>
> To *sustain* a bandwidth, you need that much data permanently in flight —
> requests issued and not yet come back. Bandwidth times latency. For this CPU,
> that's about four kilobytes. For the A4000, two hundred kilobytes. For an H100,
> two megabytes — continuously, just to keep the bus busy.
>
> A CPU core can track something like a dozen outstanding cache misses. Fourteen
> cores gets you a few hundred. It is not physically capable of holding two
> megabytes in the air, so it could not use that bandwidth even if you handed it
> the bus.
>
> [PAUSE]
>
> Now look back at the last two slides, because they just explained themselves.
> Why does an SM keep dozens of warps resident? So each one can have loads
> outstanding. Why is the register file bigger than the L1 cache — which never
> happens on a CPU? Because a warp waiting on memory keeps its state in registers,
> so parking it and swapping in another costs zero cycles.
>
> The GPU does not avoid memory latency. It hides it behind other work. That is
> the whole machine.

**If the room looks lost, one line then move on:**

> A CPU is a chef who puts one dish in the oven and waits for it. A GPU is a
> kitchen with sixty ovens — every oven is slow, but something is always coming
> out.

## Slide 5 — Which explains our 96% and 71%

**Picture:** reuse `figures/03_cpu_vs_gpu.png` from section 1 — bring the same
bars back up — or just the four bullets on a plain slide. Recognition is the
point: they have seen this number already.

**Bullets**
- Our kernel loads scalar floats: 32 lanes × 4 B = **128 B in flight per warp**
- A4000: needs ~1,600 warps to saturate, holds **~2,300** ✓ → **measured 96%**
- H100: needs ~17,000 warps, holds **~8,400** ✗ → **measured 71%**
- Fix isn't more threads — it's **more bytes per thread** (vector loads)

**Script** *(~90 s)*

> And this predicts something we measured in section one, before I told you why.
>
> Our kernel loads plain floats. Thirty-two lanes, four bytes each — a hundred and
> twenty-eight bytes outstanding per warp.
>
> On the A4000, you need about sixteen hundred warps in flight to saturate the
> bus. The card holds around twenty-three hundred. It fits comfortably — and we
> measured ninety-six percent of peak memory bandwidth.
>
> On an H100 you'd need about seventeen thousand warps, and it holds around eight
> thousand. It does not fit. And more threads won't rescue you, because the limit
> is how many warps are *resident*, not how many you launch. What you need is more
> bytes in the air per thread — vector loads instead of scalar ones.
>
> [PAUSE]
>
> We measured seventy-one percent on the GH200. That's the gap. And that's what
> it is.

> ⚠️ Verify the warp-residency figures against the whitepaper before presenting.
> The argument tolerates a 20% error; don't quote them as exact.

## Delivery notes

- **~9.5 minutes** at a measured pace, leaving buffer in a 10-minute slot.
- **The three pauses that carry the section:** the two die pictures (slide 1),
  "GPU latency is *worse*" (slide 4), and the 96/71 reveal (slide 5).
- **Numbers to know cold:** 32 (warp), ~132 (SMs), ~2 MB (in flight for H100),
  96% and 71%. Everything else can be read off the slide.
- **If running short:** compress slide 1 to one sentence over the picture; drop
  the divergence half of slide 3 (keep coalescing — section 3 needs it). Never
  cut slide 4 or 5.
- **Rehearse slide 3 most.** It is the one idea the audience has no prior hook
  for, and if it doesn't land, slide 4 doesn't either.

### Questions this section draws

**"Why not just add memory channels to the CPU?"**
You can, and server CPUs do — 8 to 12 channels gets you into the hundreds of
GB/s. But you also need the outstanding-request capacity to use them, which is
the harder half. That's why bandwidth-per-core is what actually differs.

**"Doesn't the GPU have caches too?"**
Yes, but tiny per thread — an H100 has [WP] a few hundred KB of L1 per SM shared
by thousands of threads. A GPU cache exists to coalesce and reuse *within* a
warp or block, not to hold a working set the way a CPU's L3 does.

**"What about tensor cores?"**
Irrelevant here, and worth saying so plainly: they accelerate dense matrix
multiply, which is compute-bound at high arithmetic intensity. Our kernel is 0.22
flops per byte. Tensor cores would sit idle.
